Research

Three directions, one question

How can intelligent systems become more inspectable, trustworthy, and useful? The three directions below approach that question from different angles — software provenance, experimental substrates, and traceable agent workflows. Each is a working program, not an established result.

Trustworthy AI

The unifying concern here is not "is this system safe?" but "can a third party inspect the evidence behind a safety claim?". This includes provenance for AI-generated artifacts (especially code), evaluation designed to surface failure rather than confirm success, uncertainty made visible to the user, and contestability — the ability to challenge a system's output through a procedure that can change it.

The methodological commitment is to publish the specification, the benchmark, and the failure cases together. A safety claim that cannot be reproduced against a published benchmark is treated as an assertion, not evidence. EvoGuard, Neuro-Continuum, and Kintsugi each approach this concern from a different angle: code provenance, interaction-state estimation, and argument fragility respectively.

New computing substrates

Silicon computation is extraordinary, but it is not the only physical substrate on which computation can occur. Biological neural networks — even at the scale of a few thousand neurons in a cortical organoid — exhibit properties that no current digital substrate replicates, including on-device learning without explicit gradient signals and energy use that is not proportional to bit transitions.

This direction does not claim that biological computing will replace silicon. It asks the more conservative question: what would an operating-system layer for such substrates look like, and what would have to be true for the question to be falsified? OrganoidOS is the artifact for this direction — a versioned open specification and a reference emulator. It does not claim any biological result; conformance is currently at L0 (reference emulator only).

Agents for real systems

The third direction is the application of agent architectures to real, consequential workflows — trading, logistics, compliance, documentation — where the cost of an opaque decision is high. The design constraint is not autonomy for its own sake but traceability: every agent decision must be reconstructable after the fact and contestable through a defined procedure.

Hanna AI is the artifact here: a six-agent swarm for petroleum and petrochemical trading. Whether such a system is deployed in any specific environment is a separate question, not claimed on this site.

The role of open specifications and experiments

Specifications and experiments are the two instruments that keep this work honest. A specification forces the idea into a form others can fork and contest. An experiment forces the specification to confront a benchmark designed to break it. Without both, a research direction drifts into advocacy.

This is why every project on this site links to a public repository, and why maturity labels are quoted from the repository rather than invented for the website. Where a repository does not support a claim, the claim is omitted.

Limitations and open questions

  • Exploratory, not established. The proposals on this site are exploratory. They are not peer-reviewed findings, validated results, or established science. They are public artifacts open to criticism and revision.
  • Biological computing is speculative. OrganoidOS is an operating-system specification and reference emulator. It does not demonstrate any biological result, and the broader claim that biological substrates will prove practically useful is not settled.
  • Agent systems inherit the limits of their components. A multi-agent system cannot be safer than the models and data it is built on; traceability is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Failure is part of the method. Several projects are designed to surface cases where their own hypothesis fails. Finding such a case is not a defeat; it is the method working as intended.
